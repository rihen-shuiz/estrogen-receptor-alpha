# Speaker notes

**Slide 1.** We linked a hand-assigned action class to every ligand in the ESR1 crystal structures and asked what the class does to helix 12. The structures fall into two groups by the position of this helix, and the question is whether function explains them.

**Slide 2.** ERα's ligand-binding domain ends in helix 12, the lid. In cl7 it closes over the ligand and builds the coactivator site; in cl4 it lies in that site. 95 of 98 agonist pairs are in cl7 and 88 of 115 antagonist pairs in cl4; partial agonists sit mostly in cl7 (65 of 78).

**Slide 3.** We compared classes only where constructs and peptide match. In cl7 antagonists sit +0.21 Å and partial agonists −0.17 Å from agonists; in cl4 antagonists and partial agonists differ by −0.02 Å. All of this is well inside the 0.91 Å that copies of one crystal already differ by.

**Slide 4.** Estradiol, estrone and estriol are steroids and agonists, so a steroid scaffold does not make an antagonist. The three steroidal antagonists carry a long side chain on the steroid, like fulvestrant or tamoxifen: it is the side chain that matters, as the next slides show.

**Slide 5.** Same view for both structures after superposition on the stable core. With the agonist, H12 packs over the pocket and the coactivator peptide binds next to it; with the antagonist, H12 lies in the groove the peptide would use.

**Slide 6.** The antagonist protrudes from the pocket towards H12 and physically blocks the closed position, so H12 has to move into the groove. This is the textbook mechanism; for 4-hydroxytamoxifen the label is certain, but some of our labels come from assay thresholds and could be wrong.

**Slide 7.** The main gain is that function labels now explain the two structural groups. The open questions are the exceptions, which mix crystal context with ligand action.
